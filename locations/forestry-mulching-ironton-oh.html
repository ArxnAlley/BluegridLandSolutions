    • What is forestry mulching? One tracked machine grinds standing brush, briars, and small trees into chips right where they stand.
    • How do you price a job? By the acre or by the day, whichever fits the ground better.
    • Are you licensed and insured? Yes, and we send a certificate of insurance before the machine leaves the shop.
    • Is brush hogging better than mulching? A mulcher takes stems up to eight or ten inches and grinds them flush; a brush hog leaves the woody stems behind.
    • Do you remove stumps? Mulching cuts at the stump and leaves the root mat holding the ground together.

Forestry Mulching in Ironton, OH

Lawrence County is ridge and hollow, top to bottom. The usable ground is on the ridge or in the bottom, and everything between them is the slope that makes clearing here its own problem.

Property around Ironton tends to come in one of two shapes: a narrow ridge top with long steep sides falling away from it, or a hollow bottom with the hill closing in from both directions. Either way the flat is limited, the slope is not, and any equipment that has to work here needs to spread its weight.

That is where mulching earns its keep in Lawrence County. A tracked carrier grinds standing growth without pulling roots, so a side slope that has been holding together since the furnace era keeps holding together. Nothing gets pushed into a pile at the bottom of the hollow, and nothing has to come back out a road that was barely wide enough going in.

If your project is really a road cut, a pad, or stump removal, that is land clearing. If it is a grown-over route rather than an area, look at trail cutting.

The Land Around Ironton

Ridge Tops, Side Slopes, and Where a Machine Can Go

Most of Lawrence County was cut over hard during the Hanging Rock iron furnace years and grew back on its own. What that left is even-aged second growth over thin soil, with sandstone close to the surface on the ridges and rock ledges showing through partway down the slopes. It is good ground to mulch and bad ground to dig.

North of town along SR 93 toward Pedro and Lake Vesuvius, and east toward Kitts Hill and Aid on SR 141, a lot of private tracts back directly onto the Wayne National Forest. Those parcels grow shut from the edges in, and the old ridge roads that once served them are usually the first thing to disappear under greenbrier and grapevine.

Rock Close to the Surface

Ridge tops here have sandstone within inches of the surface in places. We run the head high enough to keep off it, which is worth knowing because it changes how low a finish we can promise on a given ridge.

Short, Sharp Side Slopes

Lawrence County slopes drop fast rather than run long. We cut across the face rather than straight up it, which is slower per acre but keeps the machine and the hillside where they belong.

Vine Under Second Growth

Greenbrier and wild grape fill every gap in an even-aged canopy. That tangle is what makes a tract feel impassable long before the trees themselves are a problem.

What Lawrence County Landowners Call Us About

A Ridge Road Grown Shut

The road up the hollow was passable ten years ago and now it is a wall of vine. Mulching a corridor back open usually costs less than people expect, because it is a narrow strip rather than an area.

A Line Against the Wayne

Tracts bordering the Wayne National Forest need the boundary respected exactly. Show us the line with flagging, pins, or an old fence, and we work to it and stop.

Hollow Bottom Full of Brush

The flattest ground you own is often the most overgrown, because it grows the fastest and floods the worst. Mulching opens it without pushing debris into the creek.

A Camp You Cannot Reach

Hunting camps get used two months a year and grow over the other ten. Access first, then the ground around the camp, usually one trip for both.
